overview: |
  <p>Tonight's recital gathers three works that stand at turning points in their composers' lives. Each is a piece of late style, not necessarily of late chronology: music written at the hinge between what a composer had already mastered and what lay just out of reach. Beethoven's Sonata in E major, Op. 109, and Schumann's Kreisleriana are separated by only twenty years, but the distance between their interior landscapes is enormous. Debussy's Estampes, published in 1903, closes the program by dissolving the Germanic tradition the first two works so forcefully define.</p>
  <p>Heard in sequence, the three works sketch an arc from revelation to obsession to evaporation. Beethoven contemplates; Schumann fractures; Debussy disperses. The recital ends not with a cadence but with a view.</p>

### Ludwig van Beethoven (1770--1827)

## Sonata in E major, Op. 109

- I\. Vivace, ma non troppo --- Adagio espressivo
- II\. Prestissimo
- III\. Gesangvoll, mit innigster Empfindung

Beethoven composed the Op. 109 sonata in the summer of 1820, in the midst of the long struggle over the *Missa solemnis* and at the beginning of what would become his final cycle of piano sonatas. The work was dedicated to Maximiliane Brentano, the teenage daughter of a family to whom Beethoven was devoted, and he described the piece to her, in a letter of uncommon tenderness, as 'a remembrance' --- something small enough to fit inside an afternoon and yet large enough to contain a life.

The opening movement is formally astonishing. It begins not with a subject but with a texture: an unbroken stream of sixteenth-notes, gently rocking between tonic and dominant, as though the piece had begun before we arrived. After only eight bars this texture is interrupted --- not developed, interrupted --- by a slow, improvisatory episode in a distant key, marked *Adagio espressivo*. These two worlds, the fluent and the arrested, alternate for the remainder of the movement without ever truly reconciling.

> **Example 1.** The opening bars: a texture, not a theme. The left hand's rocking figure sustains the harmony while the right hand sings above it --- a device Beethoven would later push to its limit in the Arietta of Op. 111.

The second movement, a compact Prestissimo in E minor, serves as the sonata's scherzo --- though the term scarcely does justice to its quality of suppressed violence. There is no trio. The movement rushes by in under three minutes and ends, almost impatiently, on a bare fifth.

What follows is the reason the sonata exists. The finale is a theme and six variations, marked *Gesangvoll, mit innigster Empfindung* --- 'songful, with the most intimate feeling.' The theme is a sarabande, slow, in triple meter, and remarkable for its harmonic candor: every chord is placed with a care that borders on reverence. Over the course of the variations Beethoven transforms the theme through dance, counterpoint, and finally a trill that seems to lift the music clear of the instrument itself. The sonata closes with the theme restated, unchanged. It is one of the most moving returns in the literature --- a reminder that in late Beethoven, transformation and fidelity are not opposites.

### Robert Schumann (1810--1856)

## Kreisleriana, Op. 16

- I\. Äußerst bewegt
- II\. Sehr innig und nicht zu rasch
- III\. Sehr aufgeregt
- IV\. Sehr langsam
- V\. Sehr lebhaft
- VI\. Sehr langsam
- VII\. Sehr rasch
- VIII\. Schnell und spielend

Schumann composed *Kreisleriana* in April 1838, during one of the most turbulent periods of his engagement to Clara Wieck. Her father had forbidden the marriage; the couple's letters had become oblique, almost coded. Schumann wrote the entire work in four days. He described it to Clara as 'quite mad,' and asked her, when she played it, to 'think of me.'

The title refers to Johannes Kreisler, a fictional Kapellmeister invented by E.T.A. Hoffmann: a musician whose genius and instability were inseparable, and in whom Schumann had long recognized a self-portrait. *Kreisleriana* is not a depiction of this figure so much as an inhabitation of him. Its eight sections alternate without preparation between two temperaments --- one furious, the other tender --- as though the piece were the transcript of a single mind arguing with itself.

> **Example 2.** The opening of No. 1: a torrent of offbeat right-hand figures above a grounded bass. The rhythmic displacement is constant, creating the impression that the music is perpetually a half-step behind its own pulse.

The inner numbers are where Schumann's formal daring is most apparent. The second piece, one of the most beautiful he ever wrote, contains two intermezzi that interrupt its lyric outer sections; the returning refrain is never quite the same. The fourth, marked *Sehr langsam*, is a chorale in B-flat major whose every phrase is cut short by a restless middle voice. Nothing in *Kreisleriana* is permitted to settle.

The final piece has troubled commentators since the nineteenth century. It is the only number in the set that does not end with a definitive gesture: it drifts off on a soft, irregular figure in the bass, leaves the melody hanging, and stops. Some have heard in this a portrait of Kreisler wandering into the night. Others have heard a refusal of conclusion. Both are true. The piece ends because there is nothing more to say --- not because anything has been resolved.

### Claude Debussy (1862--1918)

## Estampes

- I\. Pagodes
- II\. La soirée dans Grenade
- III\. Jardins sous la pluie

*Estampes* --- 'prints' --- was composed in the summer of 1903 and published that autumn. The title is a painter's term, and it is the first clear signal Debussy ever gave that the piano, for him, would be an instrument for producing images rather than expressing feelings. Each of the three pieces is named for a place, but the places are imagined: Debussy had never been to Java, and would not visit Spain until the following decade. The material is not landscape but the echo of landscape in a mind furnished by museums, expositions, and books.

The first piece, *Pagodes*, grew out of the gamelan ensembles Debussy had heard at the 1889 Paris Exposition Universelle. Its harmonic material is built from the pentatonic scale; its texture is stratified into three or four simultaneous layers, each moving at its own tempo, each suggestive of a different instrument --- bell, gong, voice. The piece has no development in the classical sense. It accumulates, and then, with great gentleness, dissipates.

> **Example 3.** The stratified opening of *Pagodes*. The bass sustains a low B; the inner voices move in pentatonic ostinati; the top voice enters last. The effect is of listening to several bells at once, each ringing in its own time.

*La soirée dans Grenade* is a habanera, but it is the strangest habanera ever written. Its rhythm is present from the first bar and continues, almost without interruption, to the last --- yet the piece does not dance. Fragments of melody appear and recede; a guitar-like figure is heard and then dismissed; a distant voice sings a cadence and is silenced. Manuel de Falla, who knew Granada well, later said that Debussy's piece captured the city more truthfully than any Spanish composer had managed.

*Jardins sous la pluie* closes the set with a rush of sixteenth-notes depicting rain on a garden. Two French folk songs --- 'Do, do, l'enfant do' and 'Nous n'irons plus au bois' --- appear briefly, half-quoted, as though a child were humming them at the window while the storm passed. The piece ends with a brilliant cadence in E major, but the effect is not triumphal. It is the sun breaking through, briefly, onto a garden that has already moved on.
