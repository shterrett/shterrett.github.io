### Dimitri Shostakovich 

## Preludes and Fugues Opus 87

The _Preludes and Fugues_ are an obvious homage to Bach's _Well Tempered Clavier_. They were composed in 1950-1951 after Shostakovich attended, adjudicated, a piano competition in Leipzig in 1950. The eventual winner of the competition, Tatiana Nikolavyeva, came prepared to play any of the 48 Preludes and Fugues from the _Well Tempered Clavier_. Shostakovich was greatly impressed, and proceeded to compose his set of _Preludes and Fugues_ for Nikolayeva, who then premiered the set in Leningrad on 23 Dec 1952.

The official reception of the cycle, which Shostakovich presented to the Soviet 
Composer's Union in early 1951, was rather negative. The union complained that
the cycle was overall too morose, nervous, and sad to properly represent the
Soviet Union. In the late summer of 1952, Nikolayeva brought the cycle to the
Composer's Union again and won permission to premiere it publicly.

**No. 1 in C major** The prelude is in three, with a dactyl rhythm throughout
(stress on the first beat; unstressed two and three). It starts with harmonies
reminiscent of Bach's C major prelude from _Well Tempered Clavier_ book 1. After
a short cadenza-like figure, the harmonies become much more dissonant, including
seventh and ninth chords. Another statement of the cadenza figure leads back
into a recapitulation of the opening that then modulates to E minor and builds
to a climax. A final statement of the cadenza figure leads into a brief coda and
a quiet cadence.

The fugue is unique in that it uses only the notes in the C major scale: no
accidentals at all, even as it modulates through keys. The opening moves from
the root to the 5th and then has the 5-6-5 movement that is common throughout
the set. It follows standard practice with each voice entering in turn until all
four voices are sounding. There are then four more statements of the subject,
moving through different keys, until a brief episode modulates back to C major
and introduces a stretto in the top two voices, offset by one measure. This
transitions immediately into a stretto in the bottom two voices offset by a
half-measure, after which the music quiets and a final statement of the fugue
subject at half-tempo ends the piece.

**No. 14 in Eb minor** The Prelude bears most of the weight of this pair, and
starts immediately with a fortissimo tremolo on octave Bbs in the left hand,
quickly dropping to piano, while the right hand intones a slow chant in 7/4 over
top. The hands switch briefly with a melody in octaves in the left hand before
the first strike of a low Eb restarts the original chant over an Eb tremolo but
with the melody a half-step higher. This proceeds which quickly quiets with the
melody again in octaves in the left hand. After increasingly dissonant tremolos,
the sound parts briefly for a series of dark chords before recalling the
opening and fading to an end.

The fugue is a dance in triple meter, but the minor key counters the levity that
the lilting rhythm implies. The counter-subject emphasizes syncopations, leaning
on the quarter-half rhythm as opposed to the subject's more traditional
half-quarter. The fugue divides neatly into four parts with the first two parts
delineated by a ritardando, while the third part ends with the beginning of the
two strettos. Here, the strettos are both a bar apart; the bass leads in the
first, while the soprano leads in the second, and they are separated by a short
two-voice statement of the subject. The climax of the fugue occurs directly
after the second stretto, and does not contain the subject directly; afterwards,
the subject is heard one final time as the fugue moves to a soft conclusion; one
final flurry of quarter notes bring the piece to a somewhat ambiguous close: no
third is present in the final chord, only the Eb octaves, though in all the
chromaticism before we only hear the minor (Gb) third.
