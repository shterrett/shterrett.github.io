On the program

This program is in part “the music I managed to learn this year”; however, there are a number of deeper threads that I discovered along the way. The first is the harmonic correspondances: the first section of the program is two wildly constrasting pieces in C major. The opening Shostakovich Prelude and Fugue defies all the stereotypes of C major, cultivating a moodiness and dissonance entirely foreign to the normal conception of a happy, carefree key; this is precisely what we get from the Mozart sonata immediately afterwards. The second section moves from Bb minor to Eb minor, a v-i cadential progression, with the ethereal final Bb minor chord of the Brahms disappearing into the violent opening Bb tremelo of the Shostakovich. And finally the Rameau is in A minor, which is the relative minor of C major: we have come full circle in a sense, but not totally unscathed.

Another interesting thread is the division of the program into two groups. The Brahms and Shostakovich pieces, from the late- and post- Romantic (the Soviet system forced Shostakovich to temper his modernism), give us unapologetic views of inner emotional turmoil. The stately Classicism and late-Baroque styles of the Mozart and Rameau, however, place their drama behind a veil of propriety.

The program proceeds as outlined above with no pause between the first Prelude and Fugue and the Mozart sonata, and no pause between the Brahms intermezzo and the second Prelude and Fugue.

Dimitri Shostakovich

Preludes and Fugues Opus 87

The Preludes and Fugues are an obvious homage to Bach’s Well Tempered Clavier. They were composed in 1950-1951 after Shostakovich attended, and adjudicated, a piano competition in Leipzig in 1950. The eventual winner of the competition, Tatiana Nikolavyeva, came prepared to play any of the 48 Preludes and Fugues from the Well Tempered Clavier. Shostakovich was greatly impressed, and proceeded to compose his set of Preludes and Fugues for Nikolayeva, who then premiered the set in Leningrad on 23 Dec 1952.

The official reception of the cycle, which Shostakovich presented to the Soviet Composer’s Union in early 1951, was rather negative. The union complained that the cycle was overall too morose, nervous, and sad to properly represent the Soviet Union. In the late summer of 1952, Nikolayeva brought the cycle to the Composer’s Union again and won permission to premiere it publicly.

No. 1 in C major The prelude is in three, with a dactyl rhythm throughout (stress on the first beat; unstressed two and three). It starts with harmonies reminiscent of Bach’s C major prelude from Well Tempered Clavier book 1. After a short cadenza-like figure, the harmonies become much more dissonant, including seventh and ninth chords. Another statement of the cadenza figure leads back into a recapitulation of the opening. Instead of the expected second half of the opening, there is a sudden modulation to E minor that then builds to a climax. A final statement of the cadenza figure leads into a brief coda and a quiet cadence.

The fugue is unique in that it uses only the notes in the C major scale: no accidentals at all, even as it modulates through keys. The opening moves from the root to the 5th and then has the 5-6-5 movement that is common throughout the set. It follows standard practice with each voice entering in turn until all four voices are sounding. There are then four more statements of the subject, moving through different keys, until a brief episode modulates back to C major and introduces a stretto in the top two voices, offset by one measure. This transitions immediately into a stretto in the bottom two voices offset by a half-measure, after which the music quiets. A final statement of the fugue subject is split between the bass in F and the treble in C to end the piece.

No. 14 in Eb minor The Prelude bears most of the weight of this pair, and starts immediately with a fortissimo tremolo on octave Bbs in the left hand, quickly dropping to piano, while the right hand intones a slow chant in 7/4 over top. The hands switch briefly with a melody in octaves in the left hand before the first strike of a low Eb restarts the original chant over an Eb tremolo but with the melody a half-step higher. This builds to a climactic set of Eb octaves, which quickly quiets, with the melody again in octaves in the left hand. The tremelo adds a third note, and gradually opens. Then the sound parts briefly for a series of dark chords before recalling the opening and fading to an end.

The fugue is a dance in triple meter, but the minor key counters the levity that the lilting rhythm implies. The counter-subject emphasizes syncopations, leaning on the quarter-half rhythm as opposed to the subject’s more traditional half-quarter. The fugue divides neatly into four parts with the first two parts delineated by a ritardando, while the third part ends with the beginning of the two strettos. Here, the strettos are both a bar apart; the bass leads in the first, while the soprano leads in the second, and they are separated by a short two-voice statement of the subject. The climax of the fugue occurs directly after the second stretto, and does not contain the subject directly; afterwards, the subject is heard one final time as the fugue moves to a soft conclusion. One final flurry of quarter notes bring the piece to a somewhat ambiguous close: no third is present in the final chord, only the Eb octaves, though in all the chromaticism before we only hear the minor (Gb) third.

Wolfgang Amadeus Mozart

Sonata in C Major K545 “Sonata Facile”

Mozart wrote this sonata in June of 1788, although it was not published until 1805, after Mozart’s death. Around the same time, Mozart wrote the Violin Sonata K547, which was nicknamed “For Beginners” (however, that appellation applies only to the violin part).

The sonata follows a standard three-movement form, with the first movement in sonata-allegro form, a slow second movement, and finishes with a rondo.

The first movement’s exposition states the main, instantly recognizable, theme in C major, and then modulates to G major, the dominant, for the second theme group. The development modulates to G minor and works its way back to the subdominant (F major), where the first theme is stated again, beginning the recapitulation. Typically, the recapitulation begins in the tonic (C major), but beginning on the subdominant was not unprecedented at this time. The second theme group is then stated in the tonic, giving the recapitulation a parallel I-V (IV-I) motion to the exposition.

The second movement is in G major, the dominant of the first movement, and opens with a singing theme which is immediately embellished. The music moves|o D major briefly, and then the embellished theme is restated exactly in G major. It then moves into minor before working its way back to the opening statement, which is followed by a brief coda.

The third movement is a rondo, and the theme is stated and immediately repeated. The main theme is heard twice more, with different material between each apperance, and then the movement closes with a brilliant flourish.

Johannes Brahms

Intermezzo Op. 117 No. 2

The Three Intermezzi Op. 117 are the second collection of his last four sets of piano pieces, which were composed between 1892 and 1893. All three intermezzi are slow pieces in minor keys; Brahms referred to them in correspondence as “the three lullabies of my grief”. The second of the set, in Bb minor, is the stormiest and most dramatic of the set. It’s in a modified sonata form, unlike the outer pieces which are both in ternary. Instead of the typical exposition structure where the A and B themes are stated in turn and then repeated, Brahms states the A theme and immediately restates it with variation; then the B theme is stated and varied. A brief development ensues, trading an inversion of the first theme in the left hand with the original in the right, and then stripping it to cascading arpeggios. The recapitulation begins with a slightly modified restatement of the opening that leads into a climax. The second theme closes returns and closes the piece with a quiet Bb minor arpeggio.

Jean-Phillipe Rameau

Gavotte and Six Doubles

Rameau was a contemporary of JS Bach, and was considered one of the most important French composers of the Baroque. He wrote multiple collections of keyboard music, and his Nouvelles Suites de Pièces de Clavecin, which comprises a Suite in A minor and a Suite in G major, was published in the late 1720s

The Gavotte and Six Doubles is the final movement of the Suite in A minor. The Gavotte is the theme, and each of the Doubles is a variation on it. They are called “Doubles” because they are intended to be played twice as fast as the Gavotte. Each of the Doubles can be characterized by how it handles sixteenth notes, and this breaks into two distinct patterns. The first pattern is which hand has the sixteenths: in the second and sixth, it’s the left; in the first and fifth, it’s the right, in the third it’s the “middle hand” (the melody is in the top of the right hand, the sixteenth notes in the bottom, and the left hand plays the bass); and in the fourth the hands alternate. The second pattern is that the sixteenths are scalar in the first three doubles and arpeggiated in four through six.
